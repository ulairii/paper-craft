## Writing lesson: An analytical paper that sells a decision

The organizing question is how to allocate inference compute, not simply whether more compute helps. The proposer/verifier distinction gives the analysis a small conceptual vocabulary; budget and difficulty then organize the results (Sections 2–3, Figs. 3/7). The paper can offer a useful finding without claiming a new algorithm.

**Borrow this move.** Present the choice a practitioner faces, identify the variables that change that choice, and arrange the results around the resulting recommendations. An analytical contribution becomes concrete when the reader can make a better decision afterward.

**Original writing illustration.** “We study when to spend a fixed budget on search and when to spend it on revision” gives the study a purpose. “We comprehensively investigate the test-time scaling landscape” does not identify the decision.

## Supporting reading notes

**Narrative and paragraphs.** Sections 2–3 (pp. 3–4) divide test-time compute into proposer and verifier dimensions, then define strategy selection under a given budget. Section 8 (p. 11) explicitly presents an analytical study; its contribution does not require packaging an existing technique as a new algorithm.

**Experimental logic.** Figure 3 (p. 6) compares search across budgets and difficulty. Figures 5 and 7 (pp. 8–9) compare parallel sampling with sequential revision. Figure 8 (p. 10) incorporates inference workload into the training/inference compute tradeoff. Changes in strategy rankings conditional on difficulty support an allocation contribution; one aggregate accuracy score cannot.

**Interpretation.** Section 3.2 (p. 4) distinguishes oracle from predicted difficulty. The cost of the 2,048 samples used to estimate difficulty is excluded from the main curves. This does not directly establish fourfold end-to-end savings for a deployable router. The revision model in Section 6.1 (pp. 7–8) is trained, so the intervention is not merely a prompt change. Degradation on easy problems at high budgets is consistent with verifier exploitation, but a few failure cases cannot identify it as the sole cause.

**Detail placement.** State budget units, the candidate strategy set, whether difficulty uses labels, and whether estimation costs are included. Appendix O/Table 1 (p. 26) shows a finite strategy grid; “optimal” must be bounded by the strategies considered. Appendix N (pp. 25–26) examines verifier transfer across generation distributions. If claiming plug-and-play transfer, this issue needs a main experiment.

**Transferable recommendation.** Use budget-by-difficulty interactions to explain when to search, revise, or scale the model. Lack of progress on the hardest problems and the advantage of larger models at high inference loads are part of the contribution's boundary; do not show only winning regions.
