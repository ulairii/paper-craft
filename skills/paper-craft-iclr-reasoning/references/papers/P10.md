## CoT applicability: answer where and why separately

**Narrative and paragraphs.** The paper first asks which tasks benefit, then which execution components explain the benefit: Section 3 synthesizes literature, Section 4 runs standardized experiments, and Section 5 separates planning from execution. Actionable scope boundaries can establish an analytical contribution without inventing an algorithm.

**Experimental logic.** Section 4 compares models and tasks before examining within-task slices. Section 5/Figs. 5–6 (pp. 8–9) compare plan+direct, plan+CoT, and plan+tool to test planning and execution. A literature synthesis is not a uniformly controlled experiment; keep these evidence levels distinct.

**Interpretation.** The study covers single-prompt CoT, not every search method, self-consistency procedure, or trained reasoning model. It cannot be reframed as showing that test-time compute only helps mathematics. Section 4.2 (p. 7) retains exceptions including StrategyQA and MuSR. The equals-sign detection in Appendix G (p. 38) includes generated CoT, making it a post-generation diagnostic rather than a free pre-generation routing feature.

**Detail placement.** Answer extraction, invalid outputs, and scoring rules affect direct-answer/CoT comparisons and need a main-text summary; complete extractor statistics appear in Appendix F.3 (p. 32). Tools do not win on every model/task combination in Appendix H/Table 20 (p. 40), so aggregate trends must not conceal exceptions. Cross-model comparisons in Appendix I (pp. 40–41) mitigate contamination concerns without proving contamination absent.

**Transferable recommendation.** Define the intervention's scope before explaining heterogeneity across tasks and instances. Planning, execution, and format compliance need different experiments; an average gain cannot identify a unique mechanism.
