## Negative results: organize the paper around confounding factors

**Narrative and paragraphs.** Section 2/Table 1 (p. 3) first defines intrinsic self-correction as reasoning without external correctness feedback at inference time. It then identifies three confounds: oracle stopping, compute budgets, and initial prompts. Subsequent sections revisit them individually, allowing readers to track why previous conclusions change.

**Experimental logic.** Tables 2–3 (p. 4) switch between oracle and intrinsic settings. Figure 1 (p. 6) separates incorrect-to-correct from correct-to-incorrect transitions. Table 7 (p. 7) compares debate with self-consistency using the same number of responses. Table 8 (pp. 7–8) strengthens the first-round prompt to test whether gains merely reflect task instructions missing from the initial attempt.

**Interpretation.** Claims should stay within the evaluated models, tasks, and feedback conditions. The title's “Cannot” must not become an assertion that future models cannot self-correct. Section 7 (p. 9) also does not cover every revision for style or safety. When comparing with P06, distinguish feedback-free testing from training with correctness rewards; these conditions are compatible.

**Detail placement.** The main text must disclose stopping rules, feedback sources, iteration counts, and initial prompts. Equal response counts are a budget proxy, not necessarily equal tokens or FLOPs. Figure 2 (p. 6) and Appendix A (pp. 13–14) show both helpful and harmful revisions, providing more explanatory value than success cases alone.

**Transferable recommendation.** A persuasive negative result eliminates alternative explanations. Retain the task scope, statistical uncertainty, and strongest counterexamples for each ineffectiveness claim; do not replace measurement with assertions about the nature of language models.
