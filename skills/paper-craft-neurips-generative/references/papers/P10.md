## Writing move: Build a speed story around structure that a general method overlooks

DPM-Solver identifies a precise missed opportunity: a general ODE solver approximates a linear term that can be integrated analytically. The derivation first separates that term, then changes variables to simplify the remaining neural-network integral. This gives the algorithm a clear reason to exist. Showing DDIM as the first-order special case connects the new solver to an established method.

## Experimental logic

Same-order Runge–Kutta comparisons in both time and log-SNR distinguish the value of exploiting structure from merely using higher order or changing variables. Fixed pretrained models isolate sampling. Quality-versus-evaluation curves establish the few-evaluation operating point; measured runtime supports the practical speed interpretation. Discrete and continuous models test portability.

## Detail placement

Keep the exact reformulation and the approximated integral distinct. Explain that an order-k solver uses k network evaluations per step. The few-evaluation recipe mixes orders, while larger budgets can use adaptive steps. Move full derivative algebra and numerical constants to the appendix, retaining the assumptions needed to understand the convergence statement.

## Interpretation

The guarantee concerns numerical approximation of a learned ODE under regularity assumptions, not perfect data generation in ten steps. Endpoint and discrete-time mapping choices affect the reported curves. Higher order is not always best at the smallest budget. The speed claim concerns sampling, not likelihood evaluation, and diffusion remains slower than one-pass generators.
