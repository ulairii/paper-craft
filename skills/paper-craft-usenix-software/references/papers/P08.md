# P08: ParmeSan: Sanitizer-guided Greybox Fuzzing

Sebastian Österlund; Kaveh Razavi; Herbert Bos; Cristiano Giuffrida. **USENIX Security 2020**. [Official paper](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/osterlund) · [Full text](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec20-osterlund.pdf).

Source locations: Sections 1–8; Listing 1; Tables 3–6; Appendix A, Tables 7–11.

## Writing move: Repurpose an existing signal and align the evidence with its new role

ParmeSan gives sanitizers a second role: identifying where fuzzing effort should go. The story moves from indiscriminate code coverage and manually selected targets to automatically selected sanitizer checks. A short IR example explains target acquisition. Dynamic distances and taint-guided mutation then become the machinery needed to use those targets effectively.

## Experimental logic

Known-bug time-to-exposure results establish usefulness, while coverage at discovery tests whether less exploration can suffice. Changing the guidance sanitizer while holding runtime detection instrumentation fixed explains why the selected signal matters. Additional indirect-call experiments place the dynamic-CFG contribution in settings where it has a reason to help.

## Detail placement

Keep automatic target acquisition distinct from manually targeted experiments. State that compilation and acquisition costs are outside reported fuzzing time, with their costs reported separately. Explain the sanitizer’s bug classes and the pruning decision. Component-removal tables belong near the interpretation, including the dependency whereby removing dynamic CFG also removes lazy sanitization.

## Interpretation

Pruning does not preserve every possible bug. A new-bug case study establishes practical relevance but is sensitive to program/version selection. The reported 47 stack-distinct findings include 37 in an older library; this is not evidence of equal gains everywhere. State actual times where percentage-speedup wording would obscure the comparison.
